About

I’m a fourth-year undergraduate at KAIST, double-majoring in Computer Science and Industrial & Systems Engineering.

Research interests

I work at the intersection of machine learning and optimization. At COMET Lab, I study cooperative games, where companies share resources and need a fair way to split the cost, and computing that fair split exactly quickly becomes impossible. I first built a neural model that approximates these fair costs closely, runs many times faster than standard sampling methods, and still works on problems ten times larger than the ones it was trained on. That made me wonder whether this approximation could be more than a way to split costs. Because it’s so much cheaper to obtain than the exact answer, could it serve as a signal for harder problems, such as deciding which companies should form groups in the first place, and lead to fairer groups? That’s what I’m focusing on now: using reinforcement learning to form the groups themselves, and trying to get a model to learn strategies that go beyond what simple heuristics can find. Along the way, the work keeps raising questions I find even more interesting than the results. When can a model trained on small problems be trusted on much larger ones? When does a learned method find genuinely better solutions, and when is it just reproducing what a heuristic would do? And when is learning needed at all, rather than an exact method or a simple rule?

I’ve also worked closely with AI outside research. In two internships, I built products on top of large language models: a multi-agent system that turns documents into slide decks, and AI tools for an investment team working with confidential financial data. The more I worked with these models, the more of my time went into making them reliable: designing prompts, routing tasks between agents, checking outputs and handling cases where a model was confidently wrong. That made me much more curious about what is actually happening inside these models and how prompting shapes their behavior. Chain-of-thought reasoning is a good example. Writing out reasoning clearly helps models perform better, but a 2025 Anthropic study, “Reasoning Models Don’t Always Say What They Think,” found that models often leave out of their written reasoning the hints that actually changed their answers. So even when a model explains itself, it’s unclear how much of that explanation reflects what really happened inside it, and that’s the kind of question I’d like to understand better.
